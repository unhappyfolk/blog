Every court has kept a fool. Lear kept one who told him, in riddles, what he would not hear; al-Naysaburi filled a book with the wise madmen of the desert, Bahlul among them, who rebuked caliphs and were forgiven only because they were thought mad. The poem might belong to that lineage, and might ask what such fools are kept for.

Perhaps for their wisdom, so long as it comes without its nightmares. The jester and the harlequin know how to soften what they see; the fool does not, or will not, and so he is exiled to the dunes. One could read him as a mind wired differently, mocked for a fragility that is really a refusal to mask.

And the court that keeps him may not be a king's at all. The "we" that sounds royal grows plainer as the poem goes on, until "Friends!" turns to the audience. Whether that audience is us is left to the reader."""

A Jester’s pop lightens the mood \
A Harlequin’s dance soothes the soul \
The fool is desolate in the far dunes \
Three hearts ache in malice endured

The loud voice dictates and rules \
The craftsman grinds and earns \
The artisan’s trinkets and stools; \
All coal for a fire where all will burn

Come O Jester and solace me \
In my direst time of need \
Speak the truth, but we forbid thee \
To spout what we can’t bear to hear

Liven up O harlequin and ease our souls \
Take us afar on your merry horse \
But show us none of your nightmares \
Show us only beauty, only fair

Fetch us the desert’s fool \
In our presence \
Lest he thinks it’s his own rule \
In his prison \
Teach us the wisdom you’ve pooled \
In your prism \
And let not what you drool \
Disturb our rhythm

Friends! How amusing are those folk \
The wretched wise fools \
They claim all knowledge as they croak \
But can’t stand a duel

Upon their fragile skulls we stand \
And their peril relieves our pain \
Keep them around in our reins \
And cut the fool who breaks his chains
